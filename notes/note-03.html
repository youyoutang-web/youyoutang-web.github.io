When Technology Works but the System Doesn’t

Notes from a low-altitude economy forum in Hong Kong

September 2026 · Hong Kong

Research note · Coordination and infrastructure

I recently attended a forum on the emerging low-altitude economy in Hong Kong. I arrived relatively late and spent much of my time talking with participants during the breaks rather than sitting through the formal sessions. This turned out to be useful. Instead of hearing only about technological development, I heard people discuss what happens when these technologies enter actual operating environments.

One conversation in particular stayed with me.

Technical capability is not operational capability

I heard about a search-and-rescue system that uses drones to detect signals from people on the ground. Technically, the capability is impressive: the system can detect signals from considerable altitude and narrow down areas where someone may be located.

But detection is not identification.

A search may produce many technically valid signals without indicating which one belongs to the missing person. Search teams may therefore need to combine these signals with other information: the person’s age, habits, likely route, physical condition, previous locations, and other contextual knowledge.

The technical system makes information available. The search team still has to determine what that information means for the particular search.

This suggests a simple distinction:

Sensing is not the same as interpretation.

The important question is not necessarily whether the technology can perform more of the task. It is what must be coordinated for its output to become actionable.

In this case, that may involve sensing technology, contextual knowledge, communication between participants, search procedures, and decision-making authority. The relevant achievement—locating a missing person—depends on the arrangement connecting these elements, not on the sensing capability alone.

When the technology works but the arrangement is incomplete

This is not necessarily a technological failure.

The sensing system may be working exactly as intended. The difficulty is that the achievement is larger than the technical function.

This distinction matters for emerging infrastructure more broadly.

A drone can fly. A sensor can detect. A platform can transmit information. An operator can control an aircraft. But none of these capabilities, individually, guarantees that a low-altitude system can operate as part of an urban or regional environment.

The capabilities have to become actionable within arrangements involving transportation systems, emergency services, regulations, buildings, organizations, communication channels, and existing routines.

Some of these arrangements may be formalized as standards or procedures. Others may remain dependent on particular people, institutional knowledge, or relationships.

The question is therefore less about whether a new layer of “coordination infrastructure” exists than about how coordination requirements are actually organized.

What does the achievement become dependent on?

This is where the low-altitude economy becomes an interesting setting for research.

Emerging systems are often developed while their surrounding arrangements are still changing. Responsibilities may be unclear. Standards may be incomplete. Different organizations may have different understandings of what needs to be coordinated.

A technical capability can therefore become available before the arrangements needed to use it are fully established.

When this happens, the coordination requirement may itself become clearer through practice. Participants may discover that they need a new procedure, a different division of responsibility, another communication channel, or additional contextual knowledge.

The arrangement changes, and so does what the achievement depends on.

This also means that a successful demonstration tells us only part of the story. A drone may complete a flight or a sensing system may successfully detect a signal under controlled conditions. The more interesting question is what the achievement becomes dependent on when those conditions no longer hold.

What I want to follow

I do not yet have enough evidence from the forum to make a broader claim about the low-altitude economy. But the conversations left me with several questions:

  • When a new technical capability becomes available, what coordination requirement does it make actionable?
  • Through what arrangement is that requirement organized?
  • Which dependencies become important for the resulting achievement?
  • Which of these dependencies are formalized, and which remain situated in particular people, relationships, or forms of knowledge?
  • When an existing arrangement becomes insufficient, how is the requirement reorganized?
  • Do different arrangements for comparable coordination requirements produce different dependency configurations?

These questions extend beyond low-altitude aviation. They may also apply to AI systems, urban infrastructure, accessibility technologies, healthcare, and cultural participation.

For now, the observation I want to carry forward is simple:

A technology can work technically without the broader arrangement being sufficient to make its capability actionable.

The research question is what happens in that gap: what must be coordinated, through what arrangement, and what does the resulting achievement become dependent on?
