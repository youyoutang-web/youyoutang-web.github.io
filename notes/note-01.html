The Hidden Infrastructure of Innovation

A research note from the 2026 Horasis Asia Meeting in Hengqin–Macau

2026 · Hengqin–Macau

Research note · Coordination and infrastructure

I attended the 2026 Horasis Asia Meeting in Hengqin–Macau, where discussions brought together people working across investment, policy, education, healthcare, and infrastructure.

I went in with a relatively simple question:

What happens after the technology is built?

Across several sessions, I noticed that the discussion often moved quickly away from technical capability itself. The difficult questions were about what happened when technologies entered organizations and existing systems: how stakeholders were aligned, how workflows changed, how rules were established, how data could be used, and how different institutions worked together.

The vocabulary varied—adoption, governance, trust, regulation, organizational change—but the underlying problem often looked similar.

The technology could do something.

The question was how that capability became actionable within an existing arrangement.

Capability is only part of the achievement

One discussion on healthcare data illustrated this clearly.

European healthcare systems have historically placed strong emphasis on secure data storage, restricted access, and institutional reliability. More recently, organizations have begun exploring the use of non-sensitive data with large language models.

The important issue was not simply whether LLMs could process the data.

Organizations still had to determine which data could be used, how privacy requirements would be respected, how outputs would be checked, and where human responsibility remained.

The technical capability therefore entered an arrangement involving data governance, professional judgment, institutional procedures, and accountability.

A similar pattern appeared in discussions of smart infrastructure.

Governments are increasingly described not only as operators of infrastructure but as organizations that establish the rules within which other actors operate. One-stop digital public services were presented as examples of integrated systems in which multiple functions and data sources become available through a common institutional arrangement.

Again, the interesting achievement is not the existence of a particular technology.

It is the coordination that makes different components usable together.

Coordination between institutions

The same issue appeared in the discussion of innovation ecosystems.

Universities were described as intermediaries between government, industry, and investment. Researchers and doctoral students may identify emerging problems, develop technical approaches, prepare proposals, communicate with policymakers, and connect research with industrial implementation.

The phrase “unsung heroes” was used to describe this role.

What interests me is not whether researchers should be understood as a special class of innovation workers. It is that the achievement of an innovation ecosystem may depend on relationships that are distributed across institutions and roles.

The researcher, the government agency, the company, and the investor may each have different responsibilities. Making a project move between them requires particular arrangements: funding mechanisms, communication channels, institutional relationships, translation between professional vocabularies, and people who can navigate across organizational boundaries.

These arrangements may be difficult to see from the perspective of the final innovation.

They become more visible when we ask what had to be coordinated for the innovation to become actionable.

The infrastructure beneath the infrastructure

This made me reconsider the phrase infrastructure.

We usually recognize infrastructure when it has a visible form: roads, ports, data centers, cloud platforms, networks, or physical facilities.

But many of the arrangements discussed at the meeting were less tangible.

They included:

  • data-access procedures;
  • regulatory frameworks;
  • cross-agency coordination;
  • institutional relationships;
  • shared standards;
  • translation between professional domains;
  • funding pathways;
  • organizational knowledge;
  • mechanisms for verifying and interpreting information.

These are not necessarily a separate category of “coordination infrastructure.” They are components of arrangements through which particular coordination requirements become actionable.

The distinction matters because the presence of an infrastructure does not tell us what achievement it supports or what that achievement depends on.

A database may exist without producing a functioning data-sharing practice.

A regulatory framework may exist without resolving how organizations coordinate in a specific situation.

A university may produce research without necessarily connecting that research to policy or commercial implementation.

The relevant question is therefore not simply whether the infrastructure has been built.

It is:

What must be coordinated for the intended achievement to become actionable through it?

After the technology works

This perspective also changes how I think about innovation.

A technology can be technically successful without the broader achievement being established.

An LLM can process information.
A digital platform can connect services.
A university can produce research.
A government can establish regulations.

But the achievement we care about is usually larger than any one of these capabilities.

It may involve a clinical decision, a functioning public service, a research collaboration, or a working innovation ecosystem.

Each depends on an arrangement that connects technical capabilities with people, institutions, knowledge, procedures, and subsequent action.

The dependency configuration may be distributed across several organizations. It may include forms of situated knowledge that are difficult to formalize. It may also rely on arrangements inherited from earlier institutional systems.

This suggests a useful research question:

When a technical capability enters an existing system, what does the resulting achievement become dependent on?

What I want to follow

The meeting did not provide enough evidence to answer this question systematically. But it gave me several directions for further observation:

  • When a new technology becomes available, what coordination requirement becomes newly actionable?
  • Through what arrangement is that requirement organized?
  • Which dependencies become important for the resulting achievement?
  • Which dependencies are formalized through institutions, and which remain distributed across people or relationships?
  • What happens when the technical capability develops faster than the surrounding arrangements?
  • When an existing arrangement becomes insufficient, how is the coordination requirement reorganized?
  • Do different arrangements for comparable requirements produce different dependency configurations?

These questions extend beyond the sectors represented at Horasis.

They are relevant whenever technical capabilities have to become actionable within existing social and institutional systems.

The meeting left me with a simple observation:

Technology creates capabilities, but capabilities do not become social achievements on their own.

Between the two is an arrangement of coordination.

That is the part of innovation infrastructure I want to keep tracing.

This note draws on observations from the 2026 Horasis Asia Meeting in Hengqin–Macau. The examples are presented as illustrative patterns from discussions at the meeting rather than as direct attribution to individual speakers.
