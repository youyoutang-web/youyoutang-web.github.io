From Cultural Resources to Strategic Capability

A reading note on Hong Kong’s proposed cultural and educational transformation

2026 · Hong Kong

Coordination · Institutional Change · Cultural Infrastructure

I recently read a commentary on how Hong Kong might respond to the cultural and educational priorities associated with China’s 15th Five-Year Plan.

The article proposes that Hong Kong move beyond its existing role as a holder and distributor of cultural and educational resources and become a strategic international hub for Chinese culture, education, talent, and knowledge production.

What interests me is less any individual policy recommendation than the logic of the transformation.

The question is not simply what Hong Kong has.

It is what those resources would need to become capable of doing.

From resources to strategic capability

The article starts from two strategic positions for Hong Kong: an international center for cultural and artistic exchange, and an international education hub with a high-level talent cluster.

It then develops a broader vision of Hong Kong as a global center for Chinese cultural education: a place where Chinese civilization could be researched, taught, exhibited, translated, published, and communicated internationally.

The proposed system spans universities, schools, museums, heritage institutions, archaeological research, publishing, digital archives, cultural and creative industries, talent programs, international students, and Greater Bay Area collaboration.

One concise version of the proposed arrangement is:

Mainland fieldwork → Hong Kong research → global dissemination.

What I find interesting is that this is not primarily a proposal to acquire more resources. It is a proposal to reorganize existing resources so that they can perform a different strategic function.

The underlying movement is therefore something like:

Resources → institutional arrangements → strategic capability

But the transition between these stages is not automatic.

A university does not become an international education hub simply by having international students. A museum does not become an international cultural platform simply by holding collections. Archaeological resources do not automatically become international research capacity.

The question is what has to be coordinated for these resources to become actionable as a strategic capability.

What happens between the institutions?

The article is relatively clear about the components of the proposed system: research centers, university programs, digital databases, museums, talent schemes, international journals, exhibitions, and cross-border research platforms.

The less visible question concerns how these components are connected.

Consider the proposed movement from mainland fieldwork to Hong Kong research and then to international dissemination.

What makes the research materials accessible across institutions?

How are research relationships established and reproduced?

How are specialized findings translated into educational or international forms?

How are museum collections connected to university curricula?

How are researchers, students, data, and materials moved across institutional and territorial boundaries?

These are not simply administrative details. They are coordination requirements that have to become actionable through particular arrangements.

The arrangements may include institutional agreements, funding pathways, mobility procedures, data-access systems, translation practices, professional networks, shared standards, and recurring organizational routines.

Some may already exist. Others may have to be created, modified, or connected.

The strategic capability described in the article therefore depends on more than the presence of its institutional components.

From components to dependency configuration

This suggests a different way of reading strategic plans.

Rather than asking only whether the proposed institutions are present, we can ask:

What does the proposed achievement become dependent on?

A functioning international cultural research system might depend on recurring cross-institutional relationships, access to materials and data, people with specialized knowledge, administrative procedures, international networks, and mechanisms for connecting research to education and public communication.

Some of these dependencies may be highly formalized. Others may remain distributed across organizations or situated in particular forms of knowledge and institutional experience.

They may also be inherited from arrangements that existed before the new strategy was proposed.

This matters because a strategic plan can specify an intended institutional configuration without specifying all of the dependencies through which that configuration becomes workable.

The gap is not necessarily evidence of failure.

It is an empirical question about how the proposed achievement would actually be organized.

When strategic roles are still being formed

The article’s proposal can therefore be read as a change in placement.

Existing cultural and educational resources would be placed within new institutional and international arrangements so that they could support a different achievement: Hong Kong’s development as an international hub for Chinese culture, education, and knowledge production.

But placement is not simply the creation of new institutions.

Existing universities, museums, networks, databases, expertise, and relationships may be appropriated, modified, connected, or reconfigured.

The resulting dependency configuration may therefore differ substantially from the one supporting their existing functions.

This is where the proposal becomes interesting as a research problem.

The strategic question is:

What arrangements would make the proposed role actionable, and what would Hong Kong become dependent on in order to sustain it?

What I would want to follow

I do not yet have enough evidence to evaluate whether the proposed transformation is institutionally feasible. What interests me is what could be traced empirically if such a transformation is pursued.

  • What coordination requirements emerge when cultural and educational resources are connected across institutions and borders?
  • Through what arrangements do those requirements become actionable?
  • Which dependencies are formalized, and which remain distributed across people, relationships, or situated knowledge?
  • Which dependencies are inherited from existing institutions and infrastructures?
  • When an existing arrangement becomes insufficient, how is the coordination requirement reconfigured?
  • Do different arrangements for comparable strategic functions produce different dependency configurations?

These questions are not limited to Hong Kong.

They apply whenever a city, institution, or government attempts to transform existing resources into a new strategic capability.

A strategy may specify what a system should become.

Tracing the arrangements through which that ambition becomes actionable allows us to ask a different question:

What must be coordinated for the proposed achievement to become possible, and what does that achievement become dependent on?

That is the part of strategic transformation that is often left unspecified—and the part I would like to examine next.

This note is an independent English-language reading of a Chinese-language commentary.
