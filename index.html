Research across settings

Touch to Listen

A minimal NFC audio infrastructure for asynchronous care coordination at home.

A four-week in-home deployment examining how physical placement, updating, and maintenance shape continued access.

The system reduces interaction to a single physical action — tapping a tagged object to listen — and uses this constraint to examine where coordination work moves when interface operation is minimized.

Empirical grounding

4-week in-home deployment
6 households · 10 older adults · 8 family members

Research focus

  • Physical placement and routine alignment
  • Distributed configuration and maintenance
  • Asynchronous care coordination
  • Breakdown, reconfiguration, and continued access

Key observations

Reducing interface interaction did not eliminate coordination work. Instead, configuration, relocation, updating, and repair became more visible across household members, physical environments, and everyday routines.

The study identified recurring patterns of stabilization, friction, delayed coordination, and unresolved drift, showing how continued access depends on whether coordination work can remain aligned over time.

AI-mediated coordination

Current research examines how AI-mediated production, translation, communication, and cultural work reorganize coordination between people, computational systems, and institutional contexts.

Rather than treating AI as a replacement for creative or organizational work, this research examines the coordination required to specify, screen, calibrate, translate, evaluate, and revise AI-mediated outputs.

Research focus

  • Human–AI specification and calibration
  • Screening and selection of generated content
  • Cultural judgment in multilingual media production
  • Translation, versioning, and revision
  • Coordination across human and computational actors

Current questions

What coordination work emerges when generation becomes easier but evaluation becomes more consequential?

How are cultural judgments distributed between people, computational systems, and organizational workflows?

What happens to coordination when the same content must be generated, translated, reviewed, and adapted across different linguistic and cultural contexts?

Regional and urban-cultural research

Ongoing research examining how cultural, spatial, and organizational arrangements are coordinated in changing urban environments.

The research follows how local resources, organizations, businesses, researchers, artists, and public-facing projects become connected — and where the coordination required to sustain these connections is situated.

Research focus

  • Coordination across local actors and organizations
  • Cultural and spatial infrastructure
  • Heritage, tourism, and urban change
  • Participation and cultural production
  • Informal coordination and organizational gaps

Current observations

Projects, resources, and decisions can move faster than the organizational infrastructure required to sustain them.

The research examines how responsibilities are assigned, negotiated, redistributed, or left without a stable organizational placement, particularly when multiple actors participate without a clear division of roles, authority, and executional responsibility.

Infrastructure coordination

An emerging research direction examining how coordination requirements are organized across large-scale infrastructure systems, and how different arrangements distribute coordination work among organizations, technologies, and operational actors.

The research extends coordination analysis beyond everyday and cultural settings to systems where coordination depends on shared information, temporal alignment, distributed decision-making, and cross-organizational infrastructure.

Research focus

  • Coordination across organizational boundaries
  • Shared information and decision infrastructure
  • Temporal coordination and operational dependencies
  • Distributed responsibility and maintenance
  • How infrastructure enables, redistributes, or relocates coordination work

Current direction

Current work uses secondary evidence to examine how formal and institutional infrastructures organize coordination across complex systems.

The research asks what coordination is required for a system to function, where that work is situated, what becomes dependent on its placement, and what capacities are needed to sustain coordination over time.

The direction is being developed through comparative analysis, with future work extending toward interviews and collaboration with infrastructure and operational actors.
